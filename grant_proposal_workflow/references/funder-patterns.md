# Funder patterns

Typical structures and review criteria for common funder types. **These are orientation only.** Rules change every cycle and differ between programs, mechanisms, and solicitations. Always confirm against the current call and policy guide, and record the version and date you checked in the requirements file.

## U.S. National Science Foundation (NSF)

**Governing documents:** the Proposal & Award Policies & Procedures Guide (PAPPG) in effect on the deadline, plus the program solicitation, which overrides the PAPPG where they differ.

**Review criteria:** Intellectual Merit and Broader Impacts, each assessed against the same review elements (potential to advance knowledge or benefit society, creativity and originality, soundness of the plan including assessment of success, qualifications of the team, adequacy of resources). Solicitations often add program-specific criteria. Reviewers rate proposals on a scale such as Excellent / Very Good / Good / Fair / Poor.

**Common components to check in the current PAPPG and solicitation:**

- Project Summary (character-limited; separate Overview, Intellectual Merit, and Broader Impacts statements)
- Project Description (page-limited; must contain a section labeled Broader Impacts and, when applicable, results from prior NSF support)
- References Cited
- Budget and Budget Justification
- Biographical sketch, Current and Pending (Other) Support, and Synergistic Activities, often prepared in SciENcv
- Facilities, Equipment and Other Resources
- Data Management and Sharing Plan
- Mentoring plan when postdocs or graduate students are funded
- Letters of collaboration (format and content are restricted; endorsements are generally not allowed)
- Collaborators and Other Affiliations information

**Watch for:** font and margin rules, including which elements may use smaller type (captions, tables, formulas; the exception and its wording vary between guide versions); what counts toward page limits (e.g., results from prior support); whether References Cited is a separate upload; whether URLs are allowed in the Project Description; prohibited content in letters; program-specific supplementary documents; submission through Research.gov or Grants.gov.

## Easy to miss with any funder

These patterns recur from cycle to cycle even though the specifics change. Look for each one in the current documents rather than assuming last cycle's answer.

- **Mid-cycle changes.** Policy notices, supplements, and FAQ updates can change requirements after the solicitation is released. Check for notices attached to the guide in effect, not only the guide itself.
- **Documents that move into the submission system.** A plan that used to be an uploaded PDF may become a structured web form, or the reverse. When it is a form, draft the answers offline, organized by the form's own fields.
- **Certifications instead of uploads.** Some requirements are met by a checkbox and an institutional certification (e.g., plans for safe working environments in off-site or field research) rather than a document. The sponsored-programs office usually certifies; confirm who does and that it is done.
- **Disclosure rules.** Research-security and conflict-of-commitment rules for foreign appointments, support, collaborators, and contractors change often and touch the biosketch, other-support documents, and the budget justification.
- **Program-specific documents and criteria.** Programs layered on a general guide often add a required letter, an extra review criterion, a different budget minimum or duration, or eligibility limits. Missing one can mean return without review, so extract them into the requirements file at setup.


## U.S. National Institutes of Health (NIH)

**Governing documents:** the Notice of Funding Opportunity (NOFO), the SF424 (R&R) application guide, and the NIH Grants Policy Statement. Check the NOFO for page limits, required attachments, and any special review criteria.

**Review criteria:** NIH has moved to a simplified framework for most research project grants. It groups criteria into Importance of the Research (significance, innovation), Rigor and Feasibility (approach), and Expertise and Resources (investigators, environment), with an overall impact score. Confirm the framework and scoring that apply to the NOFO you are using.

**Common components:**

- Specific Aims (usually one page)
- Research Strategy (page limit depends on the activity code)
- Project Summary/Abstract and Project Narrative
- Bibliography and References Cited
- Biosketches (common forms, often via SciENcv), Other Support
- Budget (modular or detailed, depending on amount) and justification
- Facilities and Other Resources, Equipment
- Human subjects, vertebrate animals, authentication of key resources, Data Management and Sharing Plan, as applicable
- Letters of support, resource sharing, multiple-PI leadership plan, as applicable
- For resubmissions, an Introduction responding to the summary statement

**Watch for:** font, margin, and line-density rules; hyperlinks; where preliminary data may appear; registration in eRA Commons, SAM.gov and Grants.gov well ahead; validation errors in ASSIST or the institution's system.

## Private foundations and institutional awards

Structures vary widely. Common features:

- An online portal with character-limited fields (summary, goals, outcomes) plus uploaded documents.
- A single total-award cap that may include indirect costs, with an indirect rate far lower than federal negotiated rates.
- Salary caps for the PI, or no PI salary.
- Criteria about impact, community or stakeholder engagement, training, leadership, and geographic or mission fit, alongside scientific merit.
- An institutional letter that must commit specific resources.
- Reference letters that must address particular qualities.
- Video or other non-text components.
- Eligibility windows (career stage, institution type, location) that must be checked against the PI's dates.

**Watch for:** whether the foundation's portal saves drafts (prepare answers offline first); whether formatting rules say non-compliant documents "may not be reviewed"; how collaborations must be labeled; whether a program officer conversation is expected or encouraged.

## Other agencies

DOE, USDA-NIFA, NASA, DoD (including DARPA), NOAA, NEH, and non-U.S. funders (ERC, UKRI, national research councils) each have their own guides, templates, and criteria. Apply the same method: find the governing documents, extract every rule into the requirements file, map criteria to sections, and verify everything against the current version.
